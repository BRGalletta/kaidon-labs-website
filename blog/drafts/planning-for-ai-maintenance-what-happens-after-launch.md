Most conversations about AI projects focus on the launch: picking the right use case, choosing a model or vendor, getting the integration built, watching the demo go well. Then the project ships, the team moves on to the next priority, and the AI tool quietly becomes part of the furniture. That last part is where a surprising number of AI initiatives start to quietly decay — not because the technology failed, but because nobody planned for what comes after "it's live."

AI systems are not "set it and forget it" software in the way a lot of traditional tools are. They depend on data that changes, models that get updated by their providers, and real-world conditions that shift underneath them. If you don't plan for maintenance before launch, you'll end up doing it reactively, usually after something has already gone wrong.

## Why AI tools need more upkeep than typical software

A traditional internal tool, once built and tested, tends to keep doing the same thing indefinitely unless someone changes the code. AI systems behave differently. A model trained or tuned on your data can drift as your business changes — new products, new customer language, new edge cases it was never shown. A tool built on a third-party model can change behavior when the provider pushes an update, even without any action on your end. And a system that looked accurate in testing can degrade quietly in production as real-world inputs stray further from what it was built and evaluated on. None of this means the technology is unreliable — it means it needs a maintenance plan the way any other production system does, just with different failure modes.

## Decide who owns it before you need them to

The most common maintenance failure isn't technical — it's organizational. A project gets built, often with outside help, and then no one internally is clearly responsible for watching it once it's running. Before launch, name an owner: someone whose job includes noticing when the tool's output quality drops, when complaints start trending in a certain direction, or when a vendor announces a change that might affect you. It doesn't need to be a full-time role. It needs to be a real one, with actual accountability, rather than an assumption that "someone will notice."

## Build in a way to check it's still working

You can't monitor what you can't see. Before launch, decide how you'll know if the tool starts underperforming: a periodic spot-check of outputs, a lightweight feedback mechanism for the people using it, a way to flag responses that seem off. This doesn't need to be an elaborate analytics dashboard on day one — even a simple, consistent habit of reviewing a sample of outputs each month will catch most problems long before they become visible to customers or costly to fix.

## Plan for updates, not just fixes

Maintenance isn't only about catching things that break. It also means occasionally refreshing what the system knows — updating a knowledge base a chatbot draws from, retraining or re-tuning a model as your data grows, adjusting prompts or logic as your process changes. Treat this the way you'd treat updating a website's content: not urgent on any given day, but neglected for too long and it starts to show. Building a light cadence for this into your calendar — quarterly, semi-annually, whatever fits the tool's importance — beats waiting until something is visibly stale.

## Know what "good enough" looks like, so you can tell when it slips

It's hard to notice decline if you never defined what healthy performance looked like in the first place. Before launch, write down — even informally — what a good outcome from the tool looks like: response accuracy, resolution rate, the kind of feedback you'd expect from users. That baseline becomes your early warning system. Without it, decline tends to get noticed only once it's severe enough that someone complains.

## Maintenance is part of the project, not an afterthought

The businesses that get the most durable value from AI treat launch as the midpoint of a project, not the end of it. Budget time and attention for the months after go-live the same way you budgeted for the build itself. It doesn't have to be expensive or complicated — it just has to be planned rather than assumed.
