Most advice on AI consulting red flags is written for the moment before you sign — the pitch, the proposal, the first few calls. That's useful, but it misses a whole category of warning signs that only show up once an engagement is actually underway. By then you've paid a deposit, cleared time on your team's calendar, and started telling stakeholders a project is in motion. Walking away costs more than it did during the sales process, which is exactly why it's worth knowing what trouble looks like once you're inside it.

## Progress Updates That Are Always Demos, Never Access

Early in a build, it's normal to see progress through curated demos — a consultant walking you through what's working so far. But if every update stays a one-way demo, weeks in, and you're never given direct access to the thing being built — a staging link, a repo, a dashboard, anything you can poke at on your own time — that's worth noticing. Demos can be staged to hide rough edges. Access can't. A partner confident in the state of the work generally has no reason to keep you at arm's length from it.

## The Definition of "Done" Keeps Moving

Scope conversations happen in every project; that's not the red flag. The red flag is when "done" for the current milestone quietly becomes "done, except for X," and then "done, except for X and Y," without anyone naming that the goalposts moved. A healthy project surfaces scope changes explicitly — here's what we found, here's how it affects the plan, here's the tradeoff — so you can make a call. An unhealthy one just absorbs delay after delay under the same deadline, hoping you won't add it up until the end.

## Your Data Becomes the All-Purpose Excuse

It's genuinely common for messy or incomplete data to slow an AI project down — that's a real and fair explanation, not a cop-out, when it's specific. The red flag isn't data problems; it's data problems that are never specific. If every delay gets attributed to "data issues" without ever naming which fields, which records, or which system is the actual bottleneck, you have no way to verify the claim or help fix it. Ask for the specifics. A partner who can't produce them when asked directly is often using "the data" as a stand-in for something else going wrong.

## Communication Gets Vaguer as Problems Get Bigger

Pay attention to the correlation between bad news and clarity. Partners who are forthright when things are going well but suddenly get hedgy, slow to respond, or hard to pin down the moment something breaks are telling you something about how they'll handle the next, bigger problem. The projects that recover well from setbacks are usually the ones where the setback got named plainly and early, not the ones where it got discovered by the client three weeks later.

## There's No Record of Decisions, Only Memory

As a project evolves, decisions get made — this feature got cut, this approach got swapped for that one, this timeline got extended for this reason. If none of that lives anywhere written down, and every disagreement about "what we agreed to" comes down to competing memories of a call from six weeks ago, that's a structural problem, not a one-off miscommunication. It's reasonable to ask for a running written log of decisions and scope changes as the project moves, even informally. A partner who resists keeping one is making it harder, not easier, to hold the engagement accountable to itself.

## You're Discouraged From Talking to the People Doing the Work

If the only voice you ever hear from is the account lead or salesperson, and every attempt to get a straight answer from whoever is actually building the thing gets redirected, that's worth a direct conversation. You don't need to run the engagement day-to-day, but you should be able to ask the people doing the work a direct question occasionally and get a direct answer back, not a filtered version of one.

## None of These Are Fatal Alone

A single instance of any of this might just be a busy week — people and projects have those. What matters is the pattern. If two or three of these stack up, raise it directly with your partner rather than letting it quietly erode your confidence in the project. Good partners respond well to a direct check-in. The ones who get defensive about it are telling you something too.
