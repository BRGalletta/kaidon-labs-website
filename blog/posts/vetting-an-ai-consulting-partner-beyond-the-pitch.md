Most advice on choosing an AI consulting partner focuses on the conversation: do they listen, do they explain things clearly, do they seem trustworthy. All of that matters. But conversations are easy to get right in a sales process — the real signal is in the structure of the engagement itself. Before you sign anything, there are a handful of concrete, checkable things that tell you more about how a partnership will actually go than any amount of rapport in a discovery call.

## Look at how they price the work

Pricing structure isn't just a budget question — it's a window into incentives. A consultant billing hourly with no estimate or cap has little reason to work efficiently toward a finish line. A consultant who insists on a large fixed fee for a vaguely scoped project is often pricing in their own uncertainty, which usually gets passed to you as change orders later. The healthiest pattern is typically a well-defined, bounded first phase — priced as a fixed amount for a fixed, narrow deliverable — followed by a separate conversation about what's next once that phase proves itself out. If a partner can't break their pricing into a phase you could walk away from without much sunk cost, that's worth noting.

## Ask who will actually be doing the work

It's common for the person running the sales process to be senior, experienced, and impressive — and for the actual day-to-day work to be handed to someone else entirely once the contract is signed. That's not automatically a problem, but you should know about it going in. Ask directly: who will be building this, how many other engagements are they juggling at the same time, and will the person you've been talking to stay involved after kickoff. Vague or evasive answers to a direct question like this are more revealing than anything said in the pitch itself.

## Favor a pilot over a leap

Rather than committing to a large engagement on the strength of a few conversations, look for a partner willing to start with a small, contained pilot — something scoped tightly enough that you can evaluate the quality of their actual work, not just their presentation skills, before committing further. A partner confident in their own ability should be comfortable proving it on a small scale first. One who resists starting small, or who only offers all-or-nothing packages, is asking you to trust a lot based on very little evidence.

## Check references the right way

If a prospective partner offers references, use the call for more than a polite confirmation that the project went fine. Ask what went wrong along the way — every real project hits friction somewhere — and how the consultant handled it. Ask whether the original scope held or whether the engagement ballooned in cost or timeline. Ask whether the reference would hire them again for a second, different project, not just whether they were happy with the first one. A consultant who can't offer any reference at all, or who can only point to anonymous or unverifiable examples, deserves extra scrutiny, not necessarily automatic disqualification — newer firms exist too — but it changes how much you should lean on trust versus structure elsewhere in the engagement.

## Read the exit terms before you read anything else

Every contract should spell out what happens if either side wants to end the engagement early — what you're entitled to keep, what ongoing support (if any) is promised, and whether you own the code, data, and documentation outright or end up dependent on the vendor to touch your own system afterward. This is the clause people skip because it feels like planning for failure before you've even started. In practice, it's one of the clearest indicators of a vendor's confidence in their own work: a partner who expects the relationship to go well has little reason to make it hard for you to leave if it doesn't.

## Structure over sentiment

None of this replaces good judgment about whether you trust the people you'd be working with — that instinct still matters. But pricing shape, staffing transparency, willingness to start small, how references are handled, and what the exit terms actually say will tell you more about how an engagement is likely to unfold than an hour of rapport-building ever will. The best partners tend to welcome these questions, because they already operate this way by default.
